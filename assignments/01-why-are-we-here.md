# Assignment 1 — Why are we here?

**Weight:** 5% · **Due:** Sunday 13 September 2026, 23:59 · **Length:** 500–1000 words

A reflection, published as a GitHub repository. The essay *is* the repository's
README.

---

## The question

You are a designer or an artist. You have enrolled in a programming course. In
2026 there are systems that will write working code from a sentence of English,
and they are getting better roughly every six months.

So: **why are we here?**

That is the essay. Not "what is programming useful for" in the abstract — why
*you*, in *your* practice, should learn to do a thing a machine will increasingly
do on request.

It is a real question and I do not think it has an obvious answer. I am not
looking for a defence of the course. A well-argued "this discipline is about to
change beyond recognition, and here is what I think survives" is a better essay
than a polite agreement.

Some directions worth pulling on — take any, or none:

- **The gap between describing and specifying.** Anyone can ask for "a generative
  poster that feels unsettling". Getting something you actually want out of that
  request is a different skill. Is that skill programming, design, or something new?
- **Reading versus writing.** If a machine writes the code, someone still has to
  know whether it is any good. What does it take to be that someone?
- **Materials.** Painters understand paint; photographers understand light.
  Ihde's argument from the lecture is that technologies are never neutral tools —
  they shape what you perceive as possible. What does not understanding your
  medium cost you?
- **The historical rhyme.** Photography was going to end painting. Synthesisers
  were going to end musicianship. Frieder Nake and Georg Nees were accused of
  making machines that would replace artists — in 1965. What actually happened,
  and is this time different?
- **Craft and authorship.** If you direct a system that produces the work, whose
  work is it? Does the answer change if you understand how the system operates?
- **The uncomfortable case.** Maybe most of what is taught in a course like this
  will be obsolete within your career. What would follow from believing that?

Specific beats general. One concrete example from your own practice, examined
properly, is worth more than three paragraphs of "technology is changing rapidly".

## What to hand in

A **public GitHub repository** on your own account, containing:

### `README.md` — the essay

500–1000 words of markdown. This is the whole essay; there is no separate
document. Use headings, links, and emphasis where they help — that is what
markdown is for, and formatting a piece of writing well is part of the exercise.
If you cite something, link it.

### `PROCESS.md` — how you used AI

**Using an AI assistant on this essay is allowed and expected.** It would be
strange to ban it in a course that teaches you to use Copilot in week 1, and
stranger still on this particular topic.

What is *not* optional is telling me about it. In `PROCESS.md`, cover:

- Which tools you used, and for what — brainstorming, structure, drafting,
  editing, translation, nothing at all.
- One thing it produced that you **kept**, and why it was good.
- One thing it produced that you **rejected**, and why it was wrong, generic, or
  not what you think.

That last point carries most of the weight. Noticing that a plausible-sounding
paragraph is not actually your argument is precisely the judgement this essay is
about. An honest `PROCESS.md` saying "I used it heavily and here is where it let
me down" scores better than a defensive one.

"I did not use any" is a perfectly good `PROCESS.md`, if it is true. Say so in a
sentence and move on.

### Sources and a bibliography

Cite what you draw on, in **APA format**, in a bibliography at the bottom of the README.
It does not count toward the word limit.

**Check that every source you cite actually exists.** If a language model handed you a
reference, verify it before you use it — confident, well-formatted, entirely fictional
citations are one of the most reliable failure modes these systems have, and an essay about
machine-written text that cites a hallucinated paper is a bad look you will not recover from.

### A history that shows you worked

Write it over several sittings and commit as you go. **A handful of commits
spread across more than one day** — drafting, restructuring, cutting, fixing a
link. Commit messages in plain language: `first draft of the photography
section`, `cut 200 words`, `fix Nake link`.

This is not busywork and it is not a word count in disguise. Working in
increments you can name — and being able to go back — is the single habit that
makes the group project survivable in week 12. The essay is a low-stakes place
to build it.

## Before you start

Watch **[Dylan Beattie — *The Art of Code*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6avJHaC3C2U)**
(about an hour). It is the best argument I know for programming as an expressive
medium rather than a technical chore, and it will give you something to agree or
disagree with.

Other things worth your time, in no particular order:

- Ada Lovelace, [Note G](https://www.fourmilab.ch/babbage/sketch.html) on Babbage's
  analytical engine — the first published algorithm, written by someone who saw further
  than the machine's own inventor about what it was for.
- [Principles behind the Agile Manifesto](https://agilemanifesto.org/principles.html) —
  is programming a craft? Read it as a document about how people work, not about software.
- Don Ihde on **mediated perception** — technologies do not exist "in themselves", only in
  relation to humans and culture. The lecture's version of this argument.
- Turing, *On Computable Numbers* — what a computer actually is, from before there were any.
